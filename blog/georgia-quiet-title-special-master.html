What Happens When a Special Master Is Appointed in a Georgia Quiet Title Action

David S. Klein
Partner
October 23, 2026
10 min read

A property owner who cannot sell, refinance, or insure a parcel because of a defect in the chain of title has a problem that a closing attorney cannot fix with a corrective deed. An heir who died without a will, a security deed that was paid off but never cancelled, a tax deed with an unresolved right of redemption, a neighbor whose fence has stood on the wrong side of the line for twenty years: each of these leaves a cloud on title that only a court can remove. In Georgia, the tool for that job is a quiet title action, and the most powerful version of that action runs through a court-appointed special master.

Our earlier article on quiet title actions generally explained why these cases are brought. This article walks through the process itself: what the petition must contain, who the special master is and what he or she actually does, how notice reaches people who cannot be found, and what the final decree accomplishes. Attorneys at Rountree Leitman Klein & Geer both prosecute these actions for clients and serve as court-appointed special masters, so we see the procedure from both sides of the bench.

Two Kinds of Quiet Title Actions in Georgia

Georgia law provides two distinct quiet title procedures, and choosing the right one at the outset matters.

Conventional quia timet

The older procedure, codified at O.C.G.A. § 23-3-40 and following, is an equitable action brought against specific, named defendants to cancel a particular instrument that clouds the petitioner’s title, such as a forged deed or a lien that has been satisfied but remains of record. It is a conventional lawsuit between identified parties. The court may appoint a special master in a conventional action, but it is not required to, and there is no right to a jury trial. A conventional action resolves the dispute between the parties before the court and no one else.

Quia timet against all the world

The second procedure, found at O.C.G.A. § 23-3-60 and following, is fundamentally different. It is an in rem proceeding, meaning the action is brought against the land itself rather than against particular people. Under O.C.G.A. § 23-3-61, any person claiming a present or future freehold estate, or a leasehold with at least five years remaining, including a holder under a tax deed, may bring a proceeding “against all the world” to establish title and to determine all adverse claims, whether the claimants are known or unknown and whether the petitioner is in possession or not. The resulting decree binds everyone, including people who were never identified.

That breadth is why the statute requires a special master. When a court is asked to extinguish the rights of persons who may never appear, the legislature built in an independent officer whose job is to make sure that everyone with a potential interest receives the notice due process requires and that the petitioner’s title is actually proven rather than merely asserted.

When the Action Against All the World Is the Right Tool

In our practice, the statutory action is typically the correct choice when any of the following is true: the record owner died and the heirs are unknown, scattered, or numerous (so-called heir property); the petitioner holds under a tax deed and needs to foreclose the right of redemption and establish marketable title; a boundary line or encroachment dispute has clouded title to a strip of land; an old security deed, judgment lien, or other encumbrance remains of record and the holder has dissolved, died, or cannot be located; or a title examiner has identified a gap in the chain of title that no living person can cure by deed. In each case the petitioner needs a judgment that is good against people who cannot be named, and only the in rem procedure delivers that.

What the Petition Must Contain

O.C.G.A. § 23-3-62 is specific about the contents of the petition, and courts have treated the requirements as more than formalities. The petition is filed in the superior court of the county where the land lies and must be verified by the petitioner. It must include a particular description of the land; a statement of the interest the petitioner claims and whether that interest rests on a written instrument, on adverse possession, or on both; a description of every adverse claim of which the petitioner has actual or constructive notice; the names and addresses of possible adverse claimants, so far as they are known; and, where the petition seeks to remove a specific cloud, the grounds on which removal is sought.

Three attachments must accompany the petition: a plat of survey of the land, a copy of the instrument or instruments on which the petitioner’s own interest is based, and copies of any instruments of record on which someone else might base an adverse claim. At the same time the petition is filed, the petitioner must record a notice of lis pendens under O.C.G.A. §§ 44-14-610 through 44-14-613, which puts the world on notice that title to the property is in litigation.

Practically, this means a quiet title action begins with two professionals before any pleading is drafted: a surveyor to prepare a current plat, and a title examiner to run the chain back far enough to identify every instrument and every person who might claim through it. Skipping either step is the most common reason these petitions stall.

Appointment of the Special Master

Once the petition, plat, and instruments are filed, O.C.G.A. § 23-3-63 directs the court to submit them to a special master. The appointment is mandatory in an action against all the world. The special master must be an attorney licensed to practice in Georgia who resides in the judicial circuit where the action is pending. In the metro Atlanta counties, courts typically maintain a list of experienced real estate litigators who accept these appointments, and the court enters an order naming the special master shortly after the petition is filed.

The special master’s fee is ordinarily paid by the petitioner, and most appointment orders require a deposit toward that fee at the outset. Petitioners should budget for it alongside the survey, the title examination, the filing fee, and the cost of publication.

Notice: The Special Master’s First Job

The special master does not simply hold a hearing. Under O.C.G.A. § 23-3-65, the first task is to review the evidence filed with the petition and determine who is entitled to notice. The statute specifically requires that adjacent landowners be included, along with every adverse claimant the petitioner knows of or reasonably should know of. The special master then causes process to issue to those persons and to “all others who may be concerned.”

Known persons with ascertainable addresses are served personally by the sheriff. For respondents who live outside Georgia or whose whereabouts cannot be determined, the judge or clerk may order service by publication, which runs once a week for four weeks within a thirty-day period in the newspaper that carries the county’s sheriff’s advertisements. The published notice must identify the court, the parties, the nature of the action, the filing date, and the date of the publication order, and it must direct the respondent to appear within thirty days. Every adverse party is then entitled to at least thirty days after completion of service to file a responsive pleading.

Two features of the notice stage deserve emphasis. First, service by publication is a last resort, not a convenience. Georgia appellate courts have set aside quiet title judgments where a petitioner published notice against a respondent who could have been located through obvious channels, because publication under those circumstances does not satisfy due process. A petitioner who wants a decree that will hold up should document the search for every known claimant. Second, where the special master finds that minors, persons under a disability, unborn persons, or unknown persons may have an interest, O.C.G.A. § 23-3-65(d) requires the appointment of a disinterested person, in the nature of a guardian ad litem, to receive notice and represent those interests at the hearing.

The Hearing and the Special Master’s Report

After proof of service is filed and any guardian ad litem has been appointed, O.C.G.A. § 23-3-66 vests the special master with “complete jurisdiction within the scope of the pleadings” to determine the validity, nature, and extent of the petitioner’s title and of every adverse interest in the land, and to remove any cloud on the title. The special master sets a hearing on reasonable notice, takes evidence, and makes findings.

The petitioner bears the burden at that hearing. In a typical case the evidence consists of the certified title examination, the recorded instruments in the chain, the plat and the surveyor’s testimony, and, where the claim rests on adverse possession, testimony from witnesses who can establish the required period of open, continuous, exclusive, and notorious possession. Respondents who have answered may cross-examine and present their own evidence. The special master then prepares a written report of findings and recommendations to the superior court judge.

Either side may demand a jury trial on any question of fact, and the special master may require one on his or her own initiative. The timing of that demand is critical: Georgia courts have held that a jury demand must be made before the special master hears the case, and a party who proceeds to the hearing without demanding a jury has submitted both the facts and the law to the special master. Any party who wants a jury should say so in the responsive pleading.

The Decree and Its Effect

Upon receiving the special master’s report, or the verdict if a jury was empaneled, the court enters a decree. O.C.G.A. § 23-3-67 requires that the decree be recorded in the office of the clerk of the superior court, and once recorded it “shall operate to bind the land affected according to the tenor thereof” and is conclusive upon all persons named in it, known or unknown. The clerk also makes a margin notation on any recorded instrument that the decree affects, cross-referencing the decree, so that future title examiners will find it.

That recorded decree is the product the petitioner has been working toward. It is what allows a title insurer to write a policy, a lender to accept the property as collateral, and a buyer to close with confidence. For a tax deed holder, it converts a defeasible interest into marketable fee simple title. For heirs, it replaces a tangle of undivided fractional interests with a single, insurable chain.

What Property Owners Should Expect

A quiet title action against all the world is not a quick process. Between the survey, the title examination, the special master’s review, four weeks of publication, the thirty-day response period, the hearing, the report, and the entry and recording of the decree, an uncontested case in the metro Atlanta superior courts commonly takes six months to a year, and a contested case takes longer. Petitioners should plan any sale or refinance accordingly.

Petitioners should also expect to invest in the front end. The cases that move efficiently are the ones in which the petition arrives with a clean plat, a thorough title examination that identifies every potential claimant, documented efforts to locate those claimants, and copies of every relevant instrument. The cases that stall are the ones in which the special master has to send the petitioner back to find a missing heir or to obtain a survey that should have been attached in the first place.

Finally, owners who receive notice of someone else’s quiet title action, whether as an adjacent landowner or as a potential claimant, should not ignore it. The decree will bind them whether or not they appear, and the thirty-day response window is short. Reviewing the petition with counsel promptly is the only way to protect an interest that may otherwise be extinguished.
